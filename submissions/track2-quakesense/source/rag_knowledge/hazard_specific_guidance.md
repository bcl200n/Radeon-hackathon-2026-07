# Road blockage and detour decisions

1. A reported road blockage should reduce that route's usable capacity in planning immediately, even before the blockage is independently confirmed, since acting late on a real blockage is more costly than acting early on one that turns out to be partial or temporary.
2. When a primary route becomes blocked, prefer a detour with confirmed egress capacity over a shorter unconfirmed alternative; an unconfirmed shortcut that turns out to be also blocked costs more time than a longer confirmed route.
3. Congestion that builds gradually at a chokepoint (a narrow street, a single bridge) should trigger rerouting before the chokepoint reaches a full stop, not after, since queued people cannot immediately reverse out of a queue once it has formed.

# Fire and secondary-hazard awareness

1. Structural fires following an earthquake often originate from gas leaks or electrical faults; avoid open flame and electrical switches in an area with a suspected gas leak, and prioritize routes away from visible smoke or fire even if this lengthens the trip.
2. Fire spread direction and speed depend on wind and fuel (built-up area density); a route that is currently clear can become unsafe if it lies downwind of a growing fire, so fire-adjacent routing should be re-evaluated more frequently than routing decisions made under no active fire.

# Night-time and low-visibility evacuation

1. Route legibility degrades sharply at night or in dust/smoke; prefer routes with continuous, recognizable landmarks over marginally shorter routes through areas that are hard to navigate without light.
2. Group movement (rather than dispersed individual movement) is safer at night for reducing fall and disorientation risk, particularly for people evacuating with children or mobility limitations.
